Frequently encountered AI business use cases

These are recurring enterprise patterns, not a universal frequency ranking. Actual priority should be based on workflow volume, business impact, feasibility, risk, and user desirability.

Decision rule: use conventional automation for stable, deterministic rules; retrieval for grounded question answering over a known corpus; analytical AI for prediction and detection; generative AI for synthesis and creation; and agents only when the work needs multistep reasoning, tools, actions, or orchestration.

Classify the use case two ways

A useful portfolio taxonomy combines the business outcome with the AI solution pattern. This prevents teams from treating every idea as “a chatbot” or “an agent.”

A balanced AI success metric stack

Measure more than activity. A healthy scorecard connects solution performance to adoption, operational outcomes, and business impact while retaining risk and cost guardrails.

1 · Solution

Does it work?

  • Task success rate
  • Accuracy / groundedness
  • Precision, recall, F1
  • Latency and availability
  • Cost per successful task
  • Human override rate
2 · Adoption

Do people use it well?

  • Eligible and active users
  • Repeat / sustained use
  • Completion and abandonment
  • Feature or workflow penetration
  • User confidence and satisfaction
  • Training-to-adoption conversion
3 · Operational

Does the process improve?

  • Cycle and handling time
  • Throughput and backlog
  • Automation / containment rate
  • Error and rework rate
  • SLA attainment
  • Escalation and exception rates
4 · Business

Is value realized safely?

  • Revenue or conversion lift
  • Validated hours saved
  • Cost avoided / unit cost
  • Customer or employee outcomes
  • Risk incidents and control coverage
  • ROI, payback, and value density

Leading versus lagging indicators: task success, latency, usage, and acceptance can reveal early problems. Cost, revenue, retention, satisfaction, and risk outcomes usually take longer. Define both, but make the lagging business outcome the ultimate decision criterion.

Recommended process: define success before development

Use this six-step sequence as an intake workshop, architecture gate, or portfolio review. The output should be a one-page value case with named owners and evidence sources.

Frame the decision

Name the business problem, affected persona, workflow, pain point, strategic objective, sponsor, and accountable process owner.

Map current state

Document volumes, time, cost, errors, handoffs, controls, exceptions, and the source systems that prove current performance.

Choose the pattern

Decide whether the need is best met by rules, analytics, retrieval, generative AI, or an agent. Define the human role and escalation boundary.

Define the metric contract

Select one primary business outcome, supporting operational and adoption measures, quality thresholds, guardrails, target, horizon, owner, and data source.

Pilot the hardest part

Use representative data and edge cases. Compare against the baseline or a control group. Test technical feasibility, desirability, safeguards, and operating cost.

Gate and operate

Make a data-based go, change, stop, or scale decision. After launch, monitor KPI performance, drift, cost, incidents, overrides, and realized value.

Agent ROI model

Translate quantified value-pillar benefits into realized value by adjusting for active adoption and the portion of saved time that becomes productive output, then compare the result with total cost of ownership.

Agent return on investment
(Sum of value-pillar benefits × Adoption rate × Recapture rate) − Total cost of ownershipTotal cost of ownership × 100

Adoption rate

Share of target users actively engaging, not merely enrolled or licensed.

Recapture rate

Share of saved time that converts to productive output. The supplied model uses a 50% Forrester baseline as an initial assumption; validate or replace it with observed evidence.

Total cost of ownership

Licensing, development, integration, change management, and maintenance.

Sum of value pillars

Quantified efficiency, quality, revenue, and strategic value.

EfficiencyTime, throughput, and operating cost
QualityAccuracy, consistency, and reduced rework
RevenueConversion, retention, and speed to market
Strategic valueRisk reduction, resilience, learning, and capability

Measurement discipline: label estimated or proxy benefits separately from validated financial benefits. Apply adoption and recapture only to the benefits they logically affect, and document the time horizon consistently for both benefits and costs.

Worked example: AI-assisted benefits document processing

An illustrative metric design for extracting and normalizing plan details from carrier-authored documents. Replace the example values with measured baselines and approved targets.

Value hypothesis

If AI extracts, maps, and validates plan information while routing uncertain fields to human review, then analysts can complete more plans with fewer manual touches without reducing accuracy or compliance.

Primary outcomeValidated hours saved = (baseline minutes − assisted minutes) × completed volume ÷ 60
Quality gateCritical-field accuracy ≥ approved threshold; 100% of low-confidence fields routed to review
Economic measureNet benefit = labor value + avoided rework − model, platform, support, and review costs

Executive readiness checklist

A proposal is ready for investment review when each statement can be answered with evidence, not optimism.

Business

  • Named sponsor and process owner
  • Specific target population and workflow
  • Baseline and value hypothesis
  • One primary outcome KPI

Evidence

  • Metric definitions and denominators
  • System of record for every KPI
  • Representative pilot data
  • Validation and review plan

Control

  • Human decision and escalation boundary
  • Security, privacy, compliance review
  • Quality and safety thresholds
  • Go, change, stop, and retirement rules
